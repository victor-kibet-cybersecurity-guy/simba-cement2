Cement Guide for First-Time Home Builders in Kenya

Building your first home starts with choosing the right cement for each construction stage, storing it properly, measuring materials consistently and curing concrete, blocks and mortar correctly.

What Should a First-Time Home Builder Know About Cement?

Do not use one cement grade, one mix ratio or one construction method blindly across every part of your house.

Match the cement grade and concrete or mortar mix to the specific stage of construction.

Your house includes foundations, columns, beams, slabs, walling, mortar, plastering and floor finishes. These elements serve different purposes and therefore need different material specifications.

Simba Cement 32.5R is suited to general residential work such as masonry, walling, plastering and mortar.

For demanding structural concrete, including heavily loaded foundations, columns, beams and slabs, Simba Cement 42.5N is the higher-grade option where the structural specification requires it.

For structural work: Follow your engineer's drawings and concrete specification. A general wheelbarrow ratio should not replace an engineered concrete design for critical structural elements.

How to Store Simba Cement Before Use

Cement starts losing quality when moisture reaches the powder before it is mixed for construction.

Keep Bags Off the Ground

Place 50kg cement bags on wooden pallets or another suitable raised surface.

Keep Away from Damp Walls

Leave separation between cement stacks and walls where moisture can enter.

Use Dry Storage

Protect cement from rain, leaks, humidity and direct ground moisture.

Inspect Before Use

Significant hard lumps or hardened sections can indicate moisture exposure.

Storage rule: Do not leave cement outside overnight under a light plastic sheet and assume it is fully protected. Prepare a dry storage space before ordering large quantities.

Understanding Cement Mixing Ratios

First-time builders often hear fundis talking about ratios such as 1:3:6 or a certain number of wheelbarrows per cement bag.

These ratios describe measured proportions of construction materials.

General concrete reference:

A nominal 1:3:6 ratio represents approximately one measured part cement, three parts sand and six parts coarse aggregate.

This type of nominal mix is used as a general reference for some non-specialized concrete applications.

Structural foundations, columns, beams and slabs should follow the concrete class and mix specified by the structural engineer.

Wheelbarrow Ratios for Masonry and Block Work

Site mixes sometimes use measured wheelbarrows to maintain repeatable proportions.

References such as five to seven wheelbarrows of suitable sand per cement bag are sometimes used for particular masonry or block-making activities.

The correct ratio depends on:

  • Wheelbarrow size
  • Sand moisture
  • Material grading
  • Block or masonry application
  • Required strength
  • Project specification
Measurement tip: If you use wheelbarrows for batching, use the same wheelbarrow and fill level throughout the job.

How to Mix Cement Correctly

Measure Your Materials

Use consistent quantities of cement, sand and aggregate according to the selected mix.

Dry Blend First

Mix cement and dry aggregates thoroughly until the mixture has an even appearance before adding water.

Use Clean Water

Use clean water free from mud, oil, organic contamination and excessive salts.

Add Water Gradually

Avoid pouring excessive water into the mixture at once.

Mix Thoroughly

Make sure cement paste is distributed consistently through the sand and aggregate.

Why Dry Mixing First Helps

Dry blending helps distribute the cement through the sand and aggregates before water starts cement hydration.

Look for a consistent mixture rather than obvious patches containing mostly cement or mostly sand.

Pouring and Compacting Concrete

Properly mixed concrete still needs correct placement and compaction.

Place Concrete Without Unnecessary Delay

Organize labour, formwork and reinforcement before mixing so concrete does not sit unused for long periods.

Compact Concrete Properly

Concrete placed around reinforcement should be compacted so major trapped air pockets are removed.

Structural columns, beams and slabs often use mechanical vibration under proper site supervision.

Avoid Excess Water During Placement

Do not keep adding water to concrete simply to make it easier to pour.

Why Wet Curing Matters

Concrete, mortar and blocks do not reach their intended performance immediately after mixing.

Cement hydration continues while adequate moisture remains available.

For first-time builders, maintaining suitable wet curing for around 14 days is a useful practical target for many concrete and block applications where the project specification supports it.

The exact curing duration depends on the cement, concrete design, construction element, weather and project specification.

Curing Concrete

Keep suitable concrete surfaces moist using an approved curing method after initial hardening.

Curing Blocks

Newly produced blocks need controlled moisture and protection from rapid drying.

Curing Plastered Walls

Plastering also benefits from protection against rapid moisture loss. Follow the curing period suited to the plaster specification and weather.

Do not confuse curing with pouring water randomly. Curing means maintaining suitable moisture conditions while avoiding damage to fresh concrete or finishes.

A Simple Cement Plan for Your First House

  • Get structural drawings before major concrete work
  • Calculate cement quantities for each construction stage
  • Choose 42.5N for demanding structural work where specified
  • Choose 32.5R for general masonry and plastering
  • Prepare dry cement storage before delivery
  • Measure materials consistently
  • Dry mix before adding water
  • Use clean water
  • Avoid excessive water
  • Compact structural concrete properly
  • Cure concrete and masonry correctly
  • Keep records of quantities and deliveries

Common First-Time Home Building Mistakes

Buying Cement Before Preparing Storage

Cement can absorb moisture while sitting on site. Prepare storage before the delivery arrives.

Choosing Cement Only by Price

Use the grade appropriate for the task rather than choosing solely based on the cheapest bag.

Using Random Wheelbarrow Measurements

Inconsistent fill levels produce inconsistent material proportions.

Adding Too Much Water

Excess water can reduce concrete and mortar quality.

Skipping Compaction

Poorly compacted concrete can develop voids and honeycombing around reinforcement.

Stopping Curing Too Early

Concrete requires suitable curing conditions during its early strength-development period.

Ignoring the Engineer's Specification

Structural foundations, columns, beams and slabs should follow approved drawings and specifications.

Planning Your Cement Order

Calculate your quantities before purchasing so you avoid repeated emergency orders or excessive stock sitting on site.

When requesting a quotation, provide:

  • Cement grade
  • Number of bags
  • Project location
  • Delivery date
  • Site access information

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Simba Cement should I use for my first house?

Simba Cement 32.5R suits general masonry, walling and plastering. Simba Cement 42.5N is the higher-grade choice for demanding structural concrete such as foundations, columns, beams and slabs where specified.

Is Simba Cement 32.5R good for walling?

Yes. It is suited to general masonry, block laying, stone walling and mortar applications.

Is Simba Cement 32.5R good for plastering?

Yes. It is suited to general plastering applications when mixed with suitable sand and controlled water.

Which cement should I use for structural foundations?

Use the cement grade and concrete class specified by the structural engineer. Simba Cement 42.5N is the higher-grade Simba option for demanding foundation concrete where specified.

What does a 1:3:6 concrete ratio mean?

It represents approximately one measured part cement, three parts sand and six parts coarse aggregate. It is a nominal reference mix and should not replace engineered structural concrete specifications.

Can I use wheelbarrows to measure sand?

Wheelbarrows are commonly used for site batching, but the same wheelbarrow and consistent fill level should be used throughout the mix.

How long should concrete be cured?

Around 14 days is a useful extended wet-curing target for many residential applications, but the actual curing requirement should follow the project specification, cement, weather and structural needs.

How should I store cement bags?

Keep cement in a dry covered area, raised on pallets and protected from rain, ground moisture and damp walls.

Should I use cement with hard lumps?

Significant hard lumps can indicate moisture exposure. Inspect the bag and cement condition carefully before considering it for construction.
